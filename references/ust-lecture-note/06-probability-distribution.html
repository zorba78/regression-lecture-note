Let’s begin with one of the simplest but most fundamental experiments in probability — the Bernoulli trial.
A Bernoulli trial is a random experiment that has only two possible outcomes — success or failure, yes or no, true or false.

Think of tossing a coin. You either get a head or a tail — there’s no middle ground.
Or think about taking an exam — you either pass or fail.

We represent the outcomes of this trial using a random variable \(X\):
\[ X = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if success occurs} \\ 0 & \text{if failure occurs} \end{cases} \]

Now, if the probability of success is \(p\), then naturally, the probability of failure is \(1-p\).
The probability mass function, or PMF, of a Bernoulli random variable is:

\[f_X(x) = p^x(1-p)^{1-x}, \quad x = \{0, 1\}.\]

It’s a compact way of saying that \(P(X=1) = p\) and \(P(X=0) = 1-p\).

From this PMF, we can also find two key characteristics: - The expected value (or mean) is \(E(X) = p\). - The variance is \(Var(X) = p(1-p)\).

Intuitively, the expectation represents the long-run proportion of successes.
If \(p = 0.7\), then in the long run, about 70% of the trials would result in success.
And the variance tells us how much uncertainty or variability we can expect around that proportion.

This simple setup — a single Bernoulli trial — becomes the building block for many other distributions we’ll study next, including the Binomial distribution.

Binomial Distribution

The Binomial distribution has three defining properties:

Important

  • Bernoulli trials are conducted \(n\) times
  • The trials are independent
  • The probability of success \(p\) does not change between trials

Definition

If \(X\) counts the number of successes in the \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials, we say that \(X\) has a binomial distribution with the p.m.f

\[ P(X = x) = f_X(x) = \binom{n}{k} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}, x=\{0, 1, \ldots, n\} \]

We write

\[ X \sim \mathrm{binom}(\mathrm{size} = n,~~\mathrm{prob} = p) \]

Now, let’s move from a single Bernoulli trial to repeating it multiple times.
The Binomial distribution describes the number of successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials, where each trial has the same probability of success, \(p\).

For example, if you flip a fair coin 10 times, the number of heads you get follows a binomial distribution with \(n=10\) and \(p=0.5\).

We write this as: \[ X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p) \] Its probability mass function is: \[ P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}, \quad x = 0, 1, \ldots, n. \]

Each component has a meaning: - \(\binom{n}{x}\) counts how many ways you can have \(x\) successes in \(n\) trials. - \(p^x\) is the probability of those \(x\) successes. - \((1-p)^{n-x}\) is the probability of the remaining failures.

The three conditions for the binomial model are: 1. Each trial has only two possible outcomes. 2. The trials are independent. 3. The probability of success \(p\) is constant across trials.

You can think of the binomial distribution as the sum of \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials.
Each \(X_i\) represents success or failure in one trial, and \(X = \sum X_i\).
This perspective helps us understand its mean and variance directly — they just add up because of independence.

In practice, this distribution is incredibly common — from counting the number of defective items in a batch, to the number of patients responding to a treatment, or even the number of emails you get in your inbox that are spam (though with some conditions that lead to a Poisson model later).

Hypergeometric Distribution

Example

Suppose that an urn contains 7 white balls and 5 black balls. Let our random experiment be to randomly select 4 balls, without replacement, from the urn. Then the probability of observing 3 white balls(and thus 1 black ball) is

\[P(3W, 1B) = \frac{\binom{7}{3}\binom{5}{1}}{\binom{12}{4}}\]

  • From where?? \(\rightarrow\) an urn with 7 white balls (\(=M\)) and 5 black balls (\(=N\))

Definition

Let \(X\) be the number of success in \(K\) samples obtained from a total of \(N\) successes and \(M\) failures, the PMF of \(X\) is

\[P(X=x) = f_{X}(x) = \frac{\binom{M}{x}\binom{N}{K-x}}{\binom{M+N}{K}}, ~~~0\leq x \leq M,~0\leq K-x \leq N\]

Now that we’ve covered the Binomial distribution, let’s move to a very similar—but slightly different—distribution: the Hypergeometric distribution.

Both describe the number of successes in a series of trials, but the key difference is whether the trials are performed with or without replacement.
- In the Binomial case, each trial is independent because we replace the item after each draw.
- In the Hypergeometric case, we do not replace what we’ve drawn.

So, the trials are dependent, and the probabilities change slightly with each draw.

Let’s define it more formally.
Suppose we have a finite population of size \(N\) that contains: - \(K\) items labeled as “successes,” and
- \(N - K\) items labeled as “failures.”

We draw \(n\) samples without replacement, and let \(X\) be the number of successes observed in our sample.
Then \(X\) follows a Hypergeometric distribution, written as:

\[X \sim \text{Hypergeometric}(N, K, n)\]

and the probability mass function (PMF) is:

\[P(X = x) = \frac{\binom{K}{x}\binom{N - K}{n - x}}{\binom{N}{n}}, \quad \max(0, n - (N-K)) \le x \le \min(n, K).\]

This formula might look complicated, but the logic is simple:

  • \(\binom{K}{x}\) counts the ways to choose \(x\) successes from \(K\) available.
  • \(\binom{N-K}{n-x}\) counts the ways to choose the remaining \(n-x\) failures.
  • \(\binom{N}{n}\) counts all possible ways to draw \(n\) samples.

So, this ratio gives the probability of observing exactly \(x\) successes in the sample.

The expectation and variance of a Hypergeometric random variable \(X\) are given by:

\[E(X) = K\frac{M}{M+N}, \quad Var(X) = K\frac{MN}{(M+N)^2}\frac{M+N-K}{M+N-1}.\]

Here’s what each term represents:
- \(M\): the number of men (or one subgroup in the population)
- \(N\): the number of women (the other subgroup)
- \(K\): the number of total infected individuals (sample size)

So, the expectation represents the expected number of “successes” (in this case, women infected),
and the variance measures how much that number fluctuates across repeated samples.

Now, let’s use a real-world context to make this more concrete.
Imagine a city with \(N\) women and \(M\) men.
We’re studying a disease, and we observe:
- \(X\): number of women infected
- \(Y\): number of men infected

We want to know:
> Given that someone is infected, what is the probability that the person is a woman?

This scenario fits naturally into a contingency table framework:

Women Men Total
Infection: Yes \(X\) \(Y\) \(X+Y\)
Infection: No \(N-X\) \(M-Y\) \((N+M)-(X+Y)\)
Total \(N\) \(M\) \(N+M\)

Here, the population is finite — we’re sampling from it without replacement.
That’s exactly the condition under which the Hypergeometric model applies.

The Hypergeometric distribution allows us to calculate probabilities such as:
> “If \(K\) people are infected, what is the probability that exactly \(x\) of them are women?”

Let’s continue the example and build toward the mathematical form of the Hypergeometric distribution.

We assume that infections occur independently within each gender group:

\[X \sim \text{Binomial}(N, p), \quad Y \sim \text{Binomial}(M, p)\]

where \(p\) is the common probability of infection.

The total number of infected individuals, \(X + Y\), then follows:

\[(X + Y) \sim \text{Binomial}(N + M, p)\]

because \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent.

Now, let’s say we know that exactly \(K\) people are infected in total.
We want to find the conditional probability distribution of \(X\) given that \(X + Y = K\).

By Bayes’ theorem:

\[P(X=x \mid X+Y=K) = \frac{P(Y=K-X)P(X=x)}{P(X+Y=K)}\]

Substituting in the Binomial forms gives:

\[P(X=x \mid X+Y=K) = \frac{\binom{M}{K-X}\binom{N}{X}}{\binom{N+M}{K}}\]

And that’s exactly the Hypergeometric distribution:

\[X \sim \text{Hypergeometric}(M, N, K)\]

This derivation is quite elegant: it shows that the Hypergeometric distribution can be viewed as the conditional distribution of two independent Binomial variables, given their sum.

In other words: - If we know the total number of infections \(K\),
- and we want to know how many came from the female subgroup (\(X\)),
the conditional distribution follows a Hypergeometric law.

Hypergeometric Distribution

Relationship to Binomial Distribution

  1. The conditional distribution of \(X\) given \(X+Y=K\) is identical to hypergeometric distribution.

  2. When \(N \rightarrow \infty\), the hypergeometric distribution converges to the binomial distribution.

Important

  • Binomial distribution \(\rightarrow\) with replacement

  • Hypergeometric distribution \(\rightarrow\) without replacement

Let’s conclude our discussion of the Hypergeometric distribution by summarizing its relationship to the Binomial distribution.

  1. The conditional distribution of \(X\) given \(X + Y = K\) is identical to a Hypergeometric distribution. This means that whenever we know the total number of successes from two independent Binomial groups, the distribution of successes in one group — given the total — follows a Hypergeometric law.

  2. When the population size becomes very large, that is, \(N \to \infty\), the sampling without replacement becomes almost the same as sampling with replacement. In this limit, the Hypergeometric distribution converges to the Binomial distribution.

In simple terms:

  • The Binomial distribution applies to independent trials (sampling with replacement).
  • The Hypergeometric distribution applies to dependent trials (sampling without replacement).

This distinction is crucial in real applications.

For example:

  • If you draw cards from a deck and replace each one after drawing, use the Binomial.
  • If you draw cards and don’t replace them, use the Hypergeometric.

Poisson Distribution

Motivation

How do we measure the probability of a given number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space?

Examples

  • Call center: number of calls per hour within a day
  • Traffic accident: number of traffic accidents during 7:00 AM to 10:00 AM per day
  • Number of customers arriving in a bank between 9:00 AM and 11:00 AM

Assumption

  • The events occurs independently (independence)
  • The events occurs with a known constant mean rate (consistency)
  • The probability of two events occurring in a very short time and very small space is 0 (non-clustering)

Let’s now discuss the Poisson distribution, which is one of the most widely used probability models for describing counts of events over time or space.

The Poisson distribution is a discrete probability distribution that expresses the probability of a given number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space if these events occur with a known constant mean rate and independently of the time since the last event.

The question we’re trying to answer is:
> “What is the probability of observing a certain number of events within a fixed time or space interval?”

Let’s look at a few examples:

  • The number of calls received at a call center per hour.
  • The number of car accidents occurring on a highway between 7 AM and 10 AM.
  • The number of customers arriving at a bank between 9 AM and 11 AM.

All these situations involve counting events that occur randomly over time, and we often find that the counts follow a Poisson process — provided certain assumptions are satisfied.

The three key assumptions of the Poisson process are:

Independence: The results occurring in a particular unit of time or space are independent of the results that occur in other non-overlapping units of time or space. For example, the number of customers who visited Bank A from 9:00 to 10:00 and the number of customers who visited Bank B during the same time are independent of each other. Furthermore, the number of customers who visited Bank A until 10:00 and the number of customers who visited Bank C from 10:00 to 11:00 are also independent of each other.

Consistency: If an event occurs with mean rate of 2 within a minute, it implies 6 events will occur during 3 minutes.

Non-clustering: The probability of two events occurring in a very short time and very small space is 0. For example, the probability of two customers arriving at the bank at the same time is 0.

Poisson Distribution

Definition

Let \(\lambda\) (\(\lambda > 0\)) be the average number of events in the time interval \([0,1]\). Let the discrete random variable \(X\) count the number of events occuring in the interval.

\[f_X(x) = P(X=x) = \frac{\lambda^x\exp(-\lambda)}{x!}, ~~~x = 0,1,2,\ldots\]

We write \(X \sim \mathrm{pois}(\mathrm{lambda} = \lambda)\)

If \(X\) counts the number of events in the interval \([0, t]\) and \(\lambda\) is the average number of occurance in unit time,

\[f_X(x) = P(X=x) = \frac{(\lambda t)^x\exp(-\lambda t)}{x!}, ~~~x = 0,1,2,\ldots\]

Under these assumptions, the number of events \(X\) that occur in a given time interval follows the Poisson distribution:

\[P(X = x) = \frac{\lambda^x e^{-\lambda}}{x!}, \quad x = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\]

Here, \(\lambda\) represents the average number of events per unit time.

We write:

\[X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\]

If we’re counting events over a longer time interval \(t\), then the mean becomes \(\lambda t\), and the probability function becomes:

\[P(X = x) = \frac{(\lambda t)^x e^{-\lambda t}}{x!}\]

So, the parameter \(\lambda t\) can be interpreted as the expected number of events over the entire observation period.

This distribution is incredibly powerful for modeling real-world processes where events occur at random but with a stable average rate.

Shapes of Poisson Distribution according to \(\lambda\)

Let’s explore some key mathematical properties of the Poisson distribution.

The expected value of a Poisson random variable \(X\) is equal to its rate parameter \(\lambda\):

\[E(X) = \lambda.\]

Interestingly, its variance is also equal to \(\lambda\):

\[Var(X) = \lambda\]

This one-to-one relationship between the mean and variance is unique to the Poisson distribution.

In practice, this property helps identify whether data may be modeled as Poisson — if the sample mean and variance are roughly equal, it’s a good indicator.

Now, let’s visualize how the Poisson distribution changes depending on the value of \(\lambda\).

When \(\lambda\) is small (say, 1 or 2), the distribution is highly skewed to the right — most of the probability mass is concentrated near zero.
As \(\lambda\) increases, the distribution becomes more symmetric and starts resembling the Normal distribution.

For instance:

  • With \(\lambda = 1\), events are rare.
  • With \(\lambda = 8\) or \(\lambda = 10\), the distribution becomes bell-shaped.

This connection between Poisson and Normal distributions is important — for large \(\lambda\), we can use a Normal approximation to simplify calculations.

Relationship to Binomial Distribution

Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are Poisson random variables: \(X\sim \mathrm{pois}(\lambda_1)\), \(Y\sim \mathrm{pois}(\lambda_2)\) and \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, then \((X+Y)\sim \mathrm{pois}(\lambda_{1} + \lambda_{2})\). The conditional distribution of \(X\) given with \((X+Y) = N\) is identical to the binomial distribution with size of \(N\) and probability of \(p=\lambda_1/(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\).

Proof

By using Bayes Theorem,

\[ P(X|X+Y = N) = \frac{P(X+Y=N|X=x)P(X=x)}{P(X+Y = N)} \]

Since \(X \perp Y\), \(Y=X-N\),

\[ P(X|X+Y = N) = \frac{P(Y=N-X)P(X=x)}{P(X+Y = N)}=\frac{\frac{\exp(-\lambda_2)\lambda_2^{N-x}}{(N-x)!}\frac{\exp{(-\lambda_1)}\lambda_1^{x}}{x!}}{\frac{\exp[-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)](\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^N}{N!}} \]

Now, let’s explore a fascinating connection between the Poisson and Binomial distributions.

Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent Poisson random variables:

\[ X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1), \quad Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_2). \]

Then, the sum \(X + Y\) also follows a Poisson distribution with parameter \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2\):

\[ X + Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2). \]

Moreover, the conditional distribution of \(X\) given that \(X + Y = N\) is identical to a Binomial distribution with parameters \(N\) and \(p = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2}\).

\[X|(X+Y=N) \sim \text{Binomial}(N, \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2})\]

This result is quite powerful because it shows how the Poisson distribution can be decomposed into Binomial components under certain conditions.

To prove this, we start with Bayes’ theorem:

\[ P(X \mid X + Y = N) = \frac{P(X + Y = N \mid X = x) P(X = x)}{P(X + Y = N)} \]

Poisson Distribution

Proof (continued)

\[\begin{aligned} P(X|X+Y = N) &= \frac{N!}{x!(N-x)!}\frac{\exp{[-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)]}\lambda_1^x\lambda_2^{N-x}}{\exp{[-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)]}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^N} \\ &= \binom{N}{x}\frac{\lambda_1^x\lambda_2^{N-x}}{(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)^N} = \binom{N}{x}\frac{\lambda_1^x\lambda_2^{N-x}}{(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)^x(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)^{N-x}} \\ &= \binom{N}{x}\left(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)^x\left(1-\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)^{N-x} \\ &= \mathrm{binom}\left(\mathrm{size}=N, \mathrm{prob}=\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right) \end{aligned}\]

Important

  • The conditional distribution of \(X\) given \(X+Y\) is identical to the binomial distribution, where \(X\sim \mathrm{pois}(\lambda_1)\) and \(Y\sim \mathrm{pois}(\lambda_2)\)

  • (Check) The binomial distribution is converge to the poisson distribution when \(N\rightarrow \infty\)

By substituting the Poisson forms and simplifying, we arrive at:

\[ P(X \mid X + Y = N) = \binom{N}{x} \left(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2}\right)^x \left(1 - \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2}\right)^{N - x} \]

This result shows that the Binomial distribution is essentially embedded within the Poisson framework.

And in the other direction, when the number of trials \(N\) is large and the probability \(p\) is small — such that \(Np = \lambda\) is fixed — the Binomial distribution converges to the Poisson distribution.

So, in summary:

  • The Poisson can be viewed as the limit of the Binomial.
  • And, conditionally, the Poisson can generate a Binomial.

Let’s now talk about one of the simplest continuous distributions — the Uniform Distribution.

Imagine that a random variable has equal likelihood to take on any value within a given range.
That’s exactly what we call a uniform distribution — because the probability is “evenly spread out.”

Mathematically, we write:

\[X \sim U(a, b)\]

or equivalently,

\[X \sim \text{unif}(\text{min}=a, \text{max}=b)\]

The probability density function (PDF) is constant between \(a\) and \(b\):

\[ f_X(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{b-a}, & a \le x \le b,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

This means that any interval of equal length within \([a,b]\) has the same probability of occurring.

If we plot this, it’s just a flat rectangle. The total area under the curve must always equal 1, since it represents a complete probability space.
So, height times width equals one:

\[ (b - a) \times \frac{1}{b - a} = 1. \]

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) increases linearly between \(a\) and \(b\):

\[ F_X(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x < a,\\ \frac{x - a}{b - a}, & a \le x \le b,\\ 1, & x > b. \end{cases} \]

So the CDF looks like a straight line rising from 0 to 1.

This is intuitive: the probability that \(X\) is less than or equal to a value \(x\) simply depends on how far \(x\) is from the lower bound \(a\) relative to the total interval width \((b - a)\).

The expected value and variance of a uniform random variable are simple to compute:

\[E(X) = \frac{a + b}{2}, \quad Var(X) = \frac{(b - a)^2}{12}.\]

That means the mean is exactly at the midpoint, and the spread depends on the square of the interval width — the wider the range, the higher the variability.

Big picture. The Normal (Gaussian) distribution models many naturally occurring measurements (heights, test scores, errors) because of aggregation of many small, independent influences. Its graph is the familiar bell curve. Two parameters determine its shape:

The normal distribution, also known as the Gaussian distribution, is one of the most widely used probability distributions in statistics. It is known for its bell-shaped curve and plays a key role in various statistical methods, thanks to the central limit theorem, which states that under certain conditions, the sum of a large number of random variables will tend to follow a normal distribution.

he normal distribution was originally motivated by the need to model natural phenomena that exhibit randomness and uncertainty. Many real-world quantities, such as people’s heights, test scores, and measurement errors, tend to cluster around a central value with smaller deviations from the average.

For example, if we were to measure the heights of thousands of people, most heights would fall near the average, with fewer people being extremely short or extremely tall. This kind of distribution pattern is observed in a wide variety of fields, from biology to finance. The normal distribution is thus crucial for modeling random variables that are influenced by many small, independent factors.

Furthermore, the central limit theorem shows that when the sum of a large number of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables is considered, the distribution of the sum tends toward a normal distribution, regardless of the original distribution of the variables. This makes the normal distribution a cornerstone of inferential statistics.

  • Location (mean, \(\mu\)): where the bell is centered.
  • Scale (standard deviation, \(\sigma\)): how spread out (wide) the bell is.

Why it matters. Interpreting \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) is essential for:

  • “How unusual is this observation?” (distance from \(\mu\) in units of \(\sigma\)),
  • Comparing individuals/groups on different scales (via standardization),
  • Approximations in sampling theory (Central Limit Theorem).

Standard Normal: the common reference.

When \(\mu=0\) and \(\sigma=1\), we obtain the standard normal:

\[\phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^2/2}, \qquad -\infty<z<\infty\]

Its CDF is

\[\Phi(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}\phi(t)\,dt,\] which has no closed form; we use software or tables.

Standardization (z-scores).
Any \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) can be transformed to \(Z\):

\[Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\ \sim\ N(0,1)\]

Interpretation: \(Z\) counts how many standard deviations an observation \(X\) is from the mean. This lets us reuse the same \(\Phi(\cdot)\) for every normal problem.

Practical workflow.

  1. Translate the probability statement about \(X\) into one about \(Z\) using \(Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma\).
  2. Evaluate with \(\Phi\): e.g. \(P(X\le a)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\),
    \[P(a\le X\le b)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\]

Symmetry & tails.

  • \(\phi(z)\) and \(\Phi(z)\) satisfy: \(\phi(-z)=\phi(z)\) and \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\).
  • These identities halve your work for two-sided probabilities.

Cautions.

  • The normal model is continuous: \(P(X=a)=0\).
  • Real data may be skewed/heavy-tailed; check assumptions (histogram/QQ-plot) before using normal-based inferences.

Exponential Distribution

Motivation

How to model the time between events that occur continuously and independently at a constant average rate (waiting time)?

Examples

  • Call center: time between message transmission
  • Finance: time between market price change
  • Manufacturing: time between machine failure

What we need?

  • Capture the uncertainty and randomness of the event occurance
  • Assumption of memoryless property: the probability of the event occurring in the next time interval is independent of the past

The exponential distribution is widely used to model the time between events that occur continuously and independently at a constant average rate. This distribution is closely linked with the Poisson process, where the focus is on modeling the occurrence of rare events over time.

The exponential distribution was developed in response to the need to model situations where we are interested in the waiting time until the next event occurs. For example, consider the problem of predicting how long you will wait for a bus to arrive, assuming the buses arrive randomly. If we know that the buses come on average every 10 minutes, but we have no further information about the schedule, how can we model the waiting time for the next bus?

This kind of problem also appears in various fields such as telecommunications (time between message transmissions), finance (time between price changes), and reliability engineering (time until a product fails). In each of these cases, we are interested in understanding the distribution of time intervals between consecutive occurrences of events.

Such a situation demands a probability distribution that: 1. Captures the uncertainty and randomness of the event occurrence. 2. Does not “remember” how much time has already passed, meaning that the probability of an event happening in the future is independent of the past.

The exponential distribution satisfies both of these requirements and is particularly useful for modeling these scenarios. It provides a mathematically simple yet powerful model for describing the time between events in a wide range of processes.

Exponential DIstribution

Definition

A random variable \(X\) has an exponential distribution and write \(X \sim \exp(\lambda)\)

  • CDF
  • Expectation and Variance
  • Shape

\[ f_X(x) = \lambda \exp(-\lambda x),~~~~x\geq0 \] where

  • \(x\): The time until the next event occurs;
  • \(\lambda\): The rate at which events occur

\[ F_X(x) = 1 - \exp(-\lambda x),~~~~x\geq0 \]

\[\begin{aligned} \mu_X &=E(X) = \frac{1}{\lambda} \\ \sigma^2_X &= Var(X) = \frac{1}{\lambda^{2}} \end{aligned}\]

Key assumptions (connect to Poisson process).

  1. Events occur independently of one another.
  2. Stationary rate: on average, \(\lambda\) events per unit time.
  3. No clumping in infinitesimal intervals (probability of two or more events in an instant is negligible).

nder these assumptions, the interarrival time \(X\) follows \(X\sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)\) with PDF

\[f_X(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x},\quad x\ge 0\]

and CDF

\[F_X(x)=P(X\le x)=1-e^{-\lambda x},\quad x\ge 0\] Interpretation of \(\lambda\).

  • \(\lambda\) is a rate (events per unit time).
  • The mean waiting time is \(E[X]=1/\lambda\); the variance is \(1/\lambda^2\).
  • So, if on average 0.2 failures per hour (\(\lambda=0.2\)), then the mean time to failure is \(1/0.2=5\) hours.

Memoryless property (defining hallmark).

For \(s,t\ge 0\),

\[P(X> s+t\mid X> s)=P(X> t)\]

The “clock” forgets the past: after waiting \(s\) with no event, the distribution of additional waiting time is the same as at time 0. This property uniquely characterizes the Exponential among continuous distributions.

Reading the shape plot.

  • The density starts at its maximum when \(x=0\) and decays exponentially.
  • Larger \(\lambda\) = steeper decay (shorter waits, smaller mean).
  • The dashed vertical line at \(x=1/\lambda\) marks the mean (and also the scale of the plot).
  • The median is \(m=\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}\) (always less than the mean because of the right tail).

Quick checks / mental math.

  • Survival function \(P(X> x)=e^{-\lambda x}\). Very handy for reliability questions.
  • Quantiles: \(x_p = F^{-1}(p) = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln(1-p)\); e.g., 90th percentile is \(-\ln(0.1)/\lambda\).

Exponential Distribution

Relationship to Poisson Distribution

Poisson process: Events occurring randomly in time or space at a constant average rate \(\lambda\).

  • Poisson distribution: how many events occur in a given interval.

  • Exponential distribution: time between consecutive events in this process.

Example

Suppose that customers arrive at a store according to a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\).

  • \(Y\): a random variable representing the number of customers arriving in a given time interval \([0,t)\) \(\rightarrow\) \(Y \sim \text{Pois}(\lambda t)\)

  • \(X\): a random variable representing the length of time interval \(\rightarrow\) \(X \sim \exp(\lambda)\)

The exponential distribution is intimately connected to the Poisson process, which models events occurring randomly over time or space at a constant average rate \(\lambda\).

Link to the Poisson process (why Exponential shows up).

If arrivals follow a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\):

  • \(N(t)\sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda t)\) counts how many events occur in \([0,t)\); - The waiting time to the next event \(X\) has Exponential\((\lambda)\).

Two sides of the same coin:

  • Counts in fixed time \(\Rightarrow\) Poisson.
  • Time between events \(\Rightarrow\) Exponential.

Equivalences to remember.

  • \(P(N(t)=0)=e^{-\lambda t}\) equals \(P(\text{no event by }t)=P(X>t)\), the survival function of \(X\).
  • Interarrival times in a Poisson process are i.i.d. Exponential\((\lambda)\).

Reliability / queueing interpretations.

  • In reliability: with constant hazard rate \(h(x)=\frac{f(x)}{1-F(x)}=\lambda\), the Exponential models components whose instantaneous failure risk is constant over time (no aging).
  • In queues: interarrival times or service times are often modeled as Exponential for analytic tractability (e.g., the \(M/M/1\) queue).

Worked examples (tie back to practice).

  1. Store arrivals at rate \(\lambda=3\) per hour.
    • Probability the next customer arrives within 10 minutes (\(1/6\) hour):

\[P(X\le 1/6)=1-e^{-3\cdot(1/6)}=1-e^{-0.5}\approx 0.393\]

  • Probability you wait more than 30 minutes (\(0.5\) hour):

\[P(X>0.5)=e^{-3\cdot 0.5}=e^{-1.5}\approx 0.223\]

  1. Median vs mean.

If \(\lambda=0.5\) per hour (mean wait \(=2\) hours), the median wait is \(m=\ln(2)/0.5\approx 1.386\) hours, less than the mean—reflecting the right tail.

Common pitfalls.

  • Using Exponential when the hazard isn’t constant (e.g., machines that wear out). Consider Weibull or Gamma in that case.
  • Mixing up rate (\(\lambda\)) and mean (\(1/\lambda\)). Always label units explicitly.

Takeaway.

When events occur at a constant average rate and independently, interarrival times are Exponential. This gives you a compact toolkit:

  • \(F(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}\), \(S(x)=e^{-\lambda x}\), \(E[X]=1/\lambda\), memoryless, and easy quantiles.

From there, you can transition smoothly to more general lifetime models when assumptions fail.

Let’s now discuss the Gamma distribution, a key generalization of the Exponential distribution.

It’s one of the most important continuous probability distributions in statistics, especially for modeling waiting times and nonnegative quantities.

1. Intuitive background

Imagine a Poisson process where events occur at a constant rate \(\lambda\).

  • The Exponential distribution gives the waiting time until the first event occurs.

  • The Gamma distribution extends this idea: it gives the waiting time until the \(\alpha\)-th event occurs.

If you sum \(\alpha\) independent Exponential(\(\lambda\)) random variables:

\[Y = X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_\alpha, \quad X_i \sim \exp(\lambda)\]

then

\[Y \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda)\]

Thus, Gamma is a sum of exponentials, where:

  • \(\alpha\) (the shape parameter) corresponds to the number of events we’re waiting for,
  • \(\lambda=1/\theta\) (the rate, inverse scale) controls how fast events occur.

2. Probability Density Function (PDF)

There are two parameterizations used in practice:

(1) Using rate parameter λ:

\[f_X(x; \alpha, \lambda) = \frac{\lambda^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\lambda x}, \quad x>0\]

(2) Using scale parameter θ:

\[f_X(x; \alpha, \theta) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\theta^{\alpha}} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-x/\theta}, \quad x>0\]

Here, \(\Gamma(\alpha)\) generalizes the factorial function, with

\[\Gamma(\alpha) = (\alpha-1)! \text{ for integer }\alpha.\]

These two forms are mathematically equivalent because λ = 1/θ.
The distinction simply depends on whether you think of “rate” (λ) or “average time per event” (θ).

3. Expectation and variance

Under the rate parameterization:

\[E[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\lambda}, \quad Var(X) = \frac{\alpha}{\lambda^2}\]

Under the scale parameterization:

\[E[X] = \alpha\theta, \quad Var(X) = \alpha\theta^2\]

Interpretation: - Increasing α shifts the distribution to the right (longer waiting times). - Increasing λ compresses it horizontally (events happen faster).

4. Shape interpretation

In the slide, α is fixed at 3 while λ varies from 1/2 to 1/5.
Here’s what happens visually:

  • For larger λ (1/2), events occur more frequently → the distribution peaks closer to 0.
  • For smaller λ (1/5), events are rarer → the distribution stretches to the right, with a longer tail.

This illustrates the trade-off between rate and spread.

The Gamma distribution becomes:

  • Exponential when α = 1
  • Chi-squared when α = ν/2 and λ = 1/2

Thus, it connects several major distributions through simple parameter changes.

5. Conceptual understanding

The Gamma distribution can be thought of as the sum of α independent exponential random variables:

\[Y = X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_\alpha, \quad X_i \sim \exp(\lambda)\]

which gives

\[Y \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha, \lambda)\]

This additive property makes Gamma useful for modeling aggregated waiting times or the time until the α-th event in many real-world systems.

6. Applications

Gamma distributions appear in:

  • Reliability engineering – time to failure for systems requiring several components to fail,
  • Queueing theory – total waiting time until α customers are served,
  • Bayesian inference – conjugate prior for exponential and Poisson likelihoods, - Hydrology & meteorology – rainfall intensity modeling.

In short, the Gamma distribution provides a flexible framework for positive, right-skewed data where the exponential assumption (α=1) may be too restrictive.

7. Key takeaway

The Gamma distribution generalizes the exponential by introducing an extra parameter (α) that controls shape.

It unifies several key probability models and connects beautifully to the Poisson process interpretation — modeling the time until the α-th event occurs.

\(\chi^2\) Distribution

Definition

A random variable \(X\) is said to have a chi-square distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, denoted as \(X \sim \chi^2(\nu)\)

  • PDF
  • Expectation and Variance
  • Shape

\[f_X(x; \nu) = \frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\nu/2\right)2^{\nu/2}}x^{\nu/2-1}\exp[-x/2],~~x\geq 0\]

\[ \Gamma(\nu/2) = \int_{0}^{\infty} t^{\nu/2-1}\exp(-t) dt,~~ z>0 \]

\[\begin{aligned} \mu_X &=E(X) = \nu \\ \sigma^2_X &= Var(X) = 2\nu \end{aligned}\]

Let’s take a closer look at the Chi-Square distribution, which plays a fundamental role in inferential statistics—especially in variance estimation and hypothesis testing.

1. Definition and connection to other distributions

A random variable \(X\) is said to follow a Chi-Square distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom if:

\[X \sim \chi^2(\nu)\]

2. PDF and parameters

The probability density function is:

\[f_X(x; \nu) = \frac{1}{2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)} x^{\nu/2 - 1} e^{-x/2}, \quad x \ge 0\]

The key parameter here is ν (nu), the degrees of freedom.
It determines both the shape and spread of the distribution.

  • When \(\nu\) is small, the distribution is highly skewed right.
  • As \(\nu\) increases, it becomes more symmetric and approaches a Normal distribution.

The mean and variance are given by:

\[E[X] = \nu, \quad Var[X] = 2\nu.\]

So as degrees of freedom increase, the distribution spreads out but also centers more around its mean.

3. Visual interpretation

In the plot shown on your slide:

  • The black curve (ν = 1) is sharply right-skewed.
  • As ν increases (e.g., ν = 5, 10), the curve flattens and shifts right.
  • For large ν, it closely resembles a normal bell shape.

This is because the Chi-Square distribution is essentially a sum of squared normal random variables, and by the central limit theorem, sums of random variables tend to normality as ν grows large.

6. Intuitive example

Imagine you take ν independent standard normal observations. Each \(Z_i^2\) represents a squared standardized deviation. Summing them gives you the total “variation” in your sample — that’s what the Chi-Square captures.

So, χ² measures how much total variation is observed relative to what’s expected under a normal model.

\[X \sim \text{Gamma}\left(\alpha = \frac{\nu}{2}, \lambda = \frac{1}{2}\right)\]

This means that all the properties of the Gamma distribution—its shape, positivity, and skewness—carry over here.

\[Z^2 \sim \chi^2(1)\]

That is, the square of a standard normal variable follows a Chi-Square distribution with 1 degree of freedom.

then their sum of squares:

\[X = \sum_{i=1}^{\nu} Z_i^2\]

follows a Chi-Square distribution with ν degrees of freedom:

\[X \sim \chi^2(\nu)\]

This interpretation provides a geometric intuition—χ² represents the sum of squared standardized deviations.

Another powerful property is additivity:

If \(X_1 \sim \chi^2(\nu_1)\) and \(X_2 \sim \chi^2(\nu_2)\) are independent,
then:

\[X_1 + X_2 \sim \chi^2(\nu_1 + \nu_2)\]

This property is extremely useful in combining independent variance estimates, such as in regression and ANOVA.

t Distribution

Definition

A random variable \(X\) is said to have a \(t\) distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, denoted as \(X \sim t(\nu)\)

  • PDF
  • Expectation and Variance
  • Shape

\[f_X(x; \nu) = \frac{\Gamma\left[ (r+1)/2 \right ]}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\Gamma(\nu/2)} \left (1 + \frac{x^2}{r} \right )^{-(\nu+1)/2}, ~~~~~ -\infty < x < \infty\]

where \(\nu > 1\) is the degrees of freedom (integer) and \(\Gamma(\cdot)\) is the gamma function.

\[\begin{aligned} \mu_X &=E(X) = 0, ~~ \nu > 1 \\ \sigma^2_X &= Var(X) = \frac{\nu}{\nu - 2}, ~~ \nu > 2 \end{aligned}\]

Let’s now discuss the Student’s t distribution, a cornerstone of inferential statistics — particularly in small-sample hypothesis testing and confidence interval estimation.

1. Definition and context

A random variable \(X\) is said to have a t distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom if:

\[X \sim t(\nu)\]

The t distribution arises naturally when we estimate a population mean but the population variance is unknown and must be estimated from the data.

It was first introduced by William Gosset, who published under the pseudonym “Student” — hence the name Student’s t distribution.

2. Probability density function (PDF)

The PDF of the t distribution is:

\[f_X(x; \nu) = \frac{\Gamma\left( \frac{\nu + 1}{2} \right)}{\sqrt{\nu \pi}\Gamma(\nu/2)} \left( 1 + \frac{x^2}{\nu} \right)^{-\frac{\nu + 1}{2}}, \quad -\infty < x < \infty\]

Here:

  • \(\nu\) controls how “heavy” the tails are.
  • \(\Gamma(\cdot)\) is the gamma function, generalizing the factorial.

3. Mean and variance

\[E[X] = 0 \quad \text{for } \nu > 1, \quad Var(X) = \frac{\nu}{\nu - 2} \quad \text{for } \nu > 2\]

That is:

  • The mean exists only if \(\nu > 1\).
  • The variance exists only if \(\nu > 2\), and decreases toward 1 as \(\nu\) grows.

4. Shape intuition

In the slide’s plot, we compare t distributions for \(\nu = 1, 2, 5, 30\):

  • ν = 1: This is the Cauchy distribution — very heavy tails, undefined mean and variance.
  • ν = 2 or 5: Still quite heavy-tailed but beginning to resemble the normal.
  • ν = 30: Nearly identical to \(N(0,1)\).

The smaller the degrees of freedom, the heavier the tails — reflecting greater uncertainty when estimating variance from small samples.

5. Interpretation

Think of the t distribution as a “fatter-tailed” version of the normal distribution.

Those heavier tails protect against underestimating variability when sample sizes are small, making it more conservative in inference.

t Distribution

Properties

  1. Symmetry around 0, tails heavier than normal distribution

  2. As \(\nu \to \infty\), the \(t\) distribution approaches the standard normal distribution:

\[t(\nu) \to N(0, 1)\]

  1. Relationship to normal and chi-square distributions: If \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\) and \(V \sim \chi^2(\nu)\) are independent random variables, then the random variable

\[T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}} \sim t(\nu)\]

  1. Application:
    • The \(t\) distribution is commonly used in hypothesis testing and confidence interval estimation when the population standard deviation is unknown and the sample size is small.
    • It is particularly useful in the context of the one-sample and two-sample \(t\) tests.

\[ T = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim t(n-1) \]

Now let’s explore the key properties and practical applications of the t distribution.

1. Symmetry and shape

The t distribution is symmetric about 0, just like the standard normal.
However, it has heavier tails, meaning there’s a higher probability of extreme values. This accounts for the extra uncertainty introduced by using the sample standard deviation instead of the true σ.

2. Limiting behavior

As \(\nu \to \infty\), the t distribution converges to the standard normal:

\[t(\nu) \to N(0, 1)\]

This is because as sample size grows, our estimate of σ becomes more accurate, and the additional uncertainty vanishes.

So, for small samples → t distribution, for large samples → normal distribution.

2. Relationship to other distributions

The t distribution is derived from the normal and chi-square distributions.

If:

\[Z \sim N(0, 1) \quad \text{and} \quad V \sim \chi^2(\nu)\]

are independent, then the ratio:

\[T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V / \nu}} \sim t(\nu)\]

This ratio form gives intuition:

  • The numerator (\(Z\)) provides standardized deviation.
  • The denominator (\(\sqrt{V / \nu}\)) introduces uncertainty from estimating variance.

When the variance is known (so \(V\) is fixed), \(T\) reduces to \(Z\).

That’s why, as the degrees of freedom \(\nu\) increase, \(t(\nu)\) approaches \(N(0,1)\).

4. Application in hypothesis testing

The t distribution is crucial when performing tests about means with unknown population variance.

For a single sample, the test statistic is:

\[T = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S / \sqrt{n}} \sim t(n - 1)\]

Here:

  • \(\bar{X}\) is the sample mean,
  • \(S\) is the sample standard deviation,
  • \(n\) is the sample size,
  • \(\mu\) is the hypothesized population mean.

This statistic is used in:

  • One-sample t-test: testing if the mean differs from a known value.
  • Two-sample t-test: comparing means of two independent groups.
  • Paired t-test: comparing before-and-after measurements on the same subjects.

5. Confidence intervals

The same distribution is used for confidence intervals for the population mean when σ is unknown:

\[\bar{X} \pm t_{\alpha/2, \, n-1} \frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}\]

where \(t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\) is the critical value from the t distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

This ensures the correct coverage probability — especially for small \(n\) — where using the z distribution would underestimate uncertainty.

6. Key takeaways

  • The t distribution models sampling variability when σ is unknown.
  • Heavier tails mean greater protection against underestimating variance.
  • As sample size increases, it approaches the normal distribution.
  • It underpins the t-tests and confidence intervals used daily in statistical analysis.

Let’s turn to the F distribution, a fundamental distribution used primarily in comparing variances across samples — especially in ANOVA and regression analysis.

1. Definition and context

A random variable \(X\) is said to follow an F distribution with \((\nu_1, \nu_2)\) degrees of freedom if:

\[X \sim F(\nu_1, \nu_2)\]

where \(\nu_1\) and \(\nu_2\) are the numerator and denominator degrees of freedom, respectively.

The F distribution is a ratio of two scaled chi-square variables, and it measures how much one sample variance deviates from another, relative to what’s expected under the null hypothesis of equal variances.

2. Probability density function (PDF)

The PDF is given by:

\[f_X(x; \nu_1, \nu_2) = \frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu_1 + \nu_2}{2}\right)} {\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu_1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu_2}{2}\right)} \left(\frac{\nu_1}{\nu_2}\right)^{\nu_1/2} \frac{x^{\nu_1/2 - 1}}{\left(1 + \frac{\nu_1}{\nu_2}x\right)^{(\nu_1 + \nu_2)/2}}, \quad x \ge 0\]

It’s defined only for nonnegative x-values and depends on both degrees of freedom.

The gamma functions here act as normalizing constants to ensure the total area equals 1

3. Mean and variance

The mean exists when \(\nu_2 > 2\) and is given by:

\[E[X] = \frac{\nu_2}{\nu_2 - 2}\]

The variance exists only when \(\nu_2 > 4\):

\[Var[X] = \frac{2\nu_2^2(\nu_1 + \nu_2 - 2)}{\nu_1(\nu_2 - 2)^2(\nu_2 - 4)}\]

As \(\nu_1\) and \(\nu_2\) increase, both mean and variance stabilize, and the distribution becomes less skewed.

4. Shape and interpretation

In the plot shown: - With small \((\nu_1, \nu_2)\), the distribution is highly right-skewed. - As degrees of freedom increase, the curve flattens and becomes more symmetric.

For example: - \((\nu_1=1, \nu_2=2)\) gives a sharply declining shape. - \((\nu_1=10, \nu_2=20)\) produces a much smoother, nearly symmetric curve.

Intuitively, smaller degrees of freedom correspond to smaller sample sizes — hence more uncertainty and more skewness.

Let \(X_1\ldots X_n \overset{i.i.d}{\sim} N(\mu_1, \sigma^2_1)\), \(Y_1\ldots Y_m \overset{i.i.d}{\sim} N(\mu_2, \sigma^2_2)\), and sample variances \(S^2_X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})^2/(n-1)\), \(S^2_Y=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(Y_j - \bar{Y})^2/(m-1)\), then

Now, let’s discuss how the F distribution is used in practical statistical applications.

1. F test for equality of variances

The F statistic fundamentally measures a ratio of variances. If the two population variances are equal, the expected F value is near 1. The further it deviates from 1, the stronger the evidence that variances differ — forming the basis for variance-based hypothesis tests.

Suppose we have two independent normal samples:

\[X_1, \dots, X_n \sim N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2), \quad Y_1, \dots, Y_m \sim N(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)\]

Let:

\[S_X^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (X_i - \bar{X})^2, \quad S_Y^2 = \frac{1}{m-1} \sum_{j=1}^{m} (Y_j - \bar{Y})^2\]

Then the statistic:

\[\frac{S_X^2 / \sigma_1^2}{S_Y^2 / \sigma_2^2} \sim F(n - 1, m - 1)\]

This allows us to test:

\[H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_1: \sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2\]

by comparing the observed ratio of sample variances to an \(F\) critical value.

2. Role in ANOVA

In Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), the F statistic compares:

\[\text{Between-group variance} \quad \text{vs.} \quad \text{Within-group variance}\]

Formally:

\[F = \frac{MS_{Between}}{MS_{Within}} \sim F(\nu_1, \nu_2)\]

If the between-group variance is significantly larger than the within-group variance, it indicates at least one group mean differs from the others.

This forms the backbone of one-way and two-way ANOVA, widely used in experimental design.

3. Role in regression analysis

In multiple regression, the F statistic tests the null hypothesis:

\[H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_k = 0\]

That is, it checks whether the explanatory variables collectively have any predictive power.

The test statistic:

\[F = \frac{(SSR / k)}{(SSE / (n - k - 1))} \sim F(k, n - k - 1)\]

compares the explained variance (SSR) to the unexplained variance (SSE).

A large F value suggests that the model explains a significant portion of variability in the outcome.

4. Relationship to other distributions

  • Chi-square connection: Both numerator and denominator variances come from chi-square distributions.
  • t connection: The square of a t variable is an F variable with (1, ν) degrees of freedom.
  • Reciprocal property: \(1/F \sim F(\nu_2, \nu_1)\).

These relationships make F a central component in the broader theory of sampling distributions.

5. Practical interpretation

In practice: - F > 1 indicates that the numerator variance is larger than the denominator variance.
- The further F moves from 1, the more evidence against equal variances.
- Because the F distribution is asymmetric, we often use one-sided or right-tail tests.

6. Key takeaways

  • The F distribution models ratios of independent variances.
  • It is right-skewed, especially for small degrees of freedom.
  • It underlies essential tools in ANOVA, regression, and variance comparison.
  • It unites the t and chi-square distributions through elegant mathematical relationships.

Ultimately, the F distribution quantifies how much relative variability exists between groups — a concept at the heart of nearly all inferential statistics.
